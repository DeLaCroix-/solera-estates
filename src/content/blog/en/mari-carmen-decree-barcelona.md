<p>The so-called <strong>Mari Carmen Decree</strong> has become, within just a few weeks, one of the most widely discussed concepts in Spain&rsquo;s rental market. It is also raising many questions among property owners and investors in Barcelona: is it a new rental law? Does it affect seasonal contracts? Does it prevent owners from recovering a property? Does it change room rentals? Is it already in force?</p>
<p>The first clarification is essential: <strong>the Mari Carmen Decree is not a municipal regulation in Barcelona, nor is it a regulation on noise or neighbourly coexistence</strong>. It is the popular name given to the recent national package of housing measures introduced following the eviction of Mar&iacute;a del Carmen Abascal, whose case received enormous public attention.</p>
<p><a href="https://soleraestates.eu/">At <strong>Solera Estates</strong>, a boutique private real estate advisory firm in Barcelona</a>, we believe it is particularly important for property owners to understand which measures are actually in force before renewing, entering into or amending a rental contract.</p>

<h2>Key summary of the Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>What it is:</strong> the popular name used to refer to the national housing measures package approved in 2026 following the case of Mar&iacute;a del Carmen Abascal.</li>
<li><strong>Regulation currently in force:</strong> Royal Decree-Law 29/2026 of 6 October, published on 7 October and in force since 8 October 2026.</li>
<li><strong>What happened to the previous decree:</strong> Royal Decree-Laws 26/2026 and 27/2026, approved on 29 September, were repealed by Congress on 2 October.</li>
<li><strong>Seasonal rentals:</strong> the requirement for a genuine, demonstrable temporary reason is strengthened. As a general rule, these contracts must exceed 31 days and not exceed 12 months, unless the temporary reason justifiably continues.</li>
<li><strong>Room rentals:</strong> these are now expressly regulated under the LAU according to the residential need they meet, and the total rent charged for rooms is limited in relation to the rent for the entire property.</li>
<li><strong>Barcelona:</strong> as it is located in a stressed residential market area, certain rent restrictions are particularly relevant.</li>
<li><strong>Rent reviews:</strong> RDL 29/2026 introduces an exceptional restriction on certain rent reviews until 31 December 2027.</li>
<li><strong>Evictions:</strong> not all evictions have been prohibited. Special suspension mechanisms are established for certain vulnerable households without alternative housing, with different rules depending on who the owner or claimant is.</li>
<li><strong>Contract extensions:</strong> RDL 28/2026 provides for a new extension regime, but it is not due to enter into force until 15 November 2026. Therefore, as of the date of this article, it should not be confused with a rule that is already in force.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What is the Mari Carmen Decree really?</h2>
<p>The <strong>Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</strong> does not have an official legal name under that designation.</p>
<p>The term emerged in the political and social context created by the case of <strong>Mar&iacute;a del Carmen Abascal</strong>, an 87-year-old woman who was evicted from the Madrid home where she had lived for decades.</p>
<p>Her case generated significant mobilisation around housing and was expressly cited by the Government in the explanatory memorandum to the new regulatory package.</p>
<p>Royal Decree-Law 29/2026 itself states that the eviction of Mar&iacute;a del Carmen Abascal prompted a social response that led to the adoption of new housing protection measures.</p>
<h3>It is not a regulation on noise or homeowners&rsquo; associations</h3>
<p>It is worth clarifying a misunderstanding that has appeared in some online publications.</p>
<p>The Mari Carmen Decree <strong>is not:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>A Barcelona coexistence ordinance.</li>
<li>A regulation on noise between neighbours.</li>
<li>A rule on renovation hours.</li>
<li>Decree 161/2010 of the Government of Catalonia.</li>
<li>A regulation created by an alleged neighbour called Mari Carmen who complained about noise.</li>
</ul>
<p>It is national legislation relating to <strong>rentals, temporary contracts, room rentals, protection against certain evictions, rents, large-scale landlords and other housing measures</strong>.</p>
<h2>Why were the so-called Mari Carmen decrees approved twice?</h2>
<p>To understand the <strong>Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</strong> correctly, we must distinguish between two moments.</p>
<h3>Initial approval: 29 September 2026</h3>
<p>The Government initially approved:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Royal Decree-Law 26/2026</strong>, containing a broad package of housing measures.</li>
<li><strong>Royal Decree-Law 27/2026</strong>, mainly focused on strengthening the stability of primary residence rental contracts.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both texts had an extremely short lifespan.</p>
<h3>Repeal on 2 October</h3>
<p>Congress agreed to repeal both Royal Decree-Laws on <strong>2 October 2026</strong>.</p>
<p>Therefore, any article currently explaining the Mari Carmen Decree using only RDLs 26/2026 and 27/2026 is out of date.</p>
<h3>New approval on 6 October</h3>
<p>A few days later, the measures were approved again through:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Royal Decree-Law 29/2026 of 6 October.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Royal Decree-Law 28/2026 of 6 October.</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>RDL 29/2026 entered into force on 8 October.</p>
<p>RDL 28/2026 provides for deferred entry into force until 15 November 2026.</p>
<h2>How the Mari Carmen Decree affects seasonal rentals in Barcelona</h2>
<p>This is probably one of the changes with the greatest practical impact for Barcelona property owners.</p>
<p>In recent years, the use of <strong>seasonal contracts</strong> has increased significantly, especially in high-demand markets such as Barcelona.</p>
<p>RDL 29/2026 seeks to prevent a temporary contract from being used to disguise a genuine need for a primary residence.</p>
<h3>There must be a genuine temporary reason</h3>
<p>A temporary housing contract must be justified by a <strong>genuine and demonstrable temporary reason</strong>.</p>
<p>It is not enough to call the document a &ldquo;seasonal contract&rdquo;.</p>
<p>There must be a reason explaining why the tenant needs the property only temporarily.</p>
<p>Depending on the case, situations may relate to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Temporary work.</li>
<li>Studies.</li>
<li>Medical treatment.</li>
<li>Professional assignments.</li>
<li>Fixed-term projects.</li>
<li>Other genuine temporary reasons that can be demonstrated.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Duration of a temporary rental</h3>
<p>The new regulation establishes that temporary rentals must exceed <strong>31 days</strong> and, as a general rule, must not exceed <strong>12 months</strong>.</p>
<p>The legislation contemplates situations in which the temporary reason may subsequently continue, so the 12-month limit should not be interpreted entirely automatically without analysing the specific case.</p>
<h3>What happens if the temporary nature is false</h3>
<p>This is one of the most important issues for property owners.</p>
<p>If there is no genuine, demonstrable temporary reason, the contract may be subject to the regime applicable to a <strong>primary residence</strong>.</p>
<p>In addition, the regulation provides for consequences where temporary contracts are unjustifiably extended or successive contracts are entered into for the same property.</p>
<p>Therefore, systematically using 11-month contracts can no longer simply be considered a contractual strategy to avoid the standard residential rental regime.</p>
<h2>How it affects room rentals in Barcelona</h2>
<p>Another particularly relevant amendment is the express regulation of <strong>room rentals</strong>.</p>
<p>The new legislation distinguishes whether the room meets a primary or temporary housing need and applies the corresponding regime.</p>
<h3>The combined rent for rooms cannot be used to increase rent without limit</h3>
<p>One of the rules introduced establishes that the sum of rents under partial or room rental contracts simultaneously in force for the same property <strong>cannot exceed the rent under a single rental contract for the entire property</strong>.</p>
<p>This rule is particularly relevant in Barcelona, where room rentals have become increasingly common.</p>
<h3>What happens in a stressed residential market area</h3>
<p>Barcelona is included among the areas declared to be stressed residential markets.</p>
<p>In these areas, the rent for the entire property and, where applicable, the combined rent for rooms must comply with the restrictions established by the applicable legislation.</p>
<p>Therefore, contractually dividing a property into four rooms does not automatically allow the restrictions applicable to the entire property to be avoided.</p>
<h2>Can the owner charge estate agency fees to the tenant?</h2>
<p>RDL 29/2026 strengthens the prohibition on passing certain costs related to entering into a contract on to the tenant.</p>
<p>The regulation establishes that <strong>property management and contract formalisation costs may not be passed on to the tenant, either directly or indirectly</strong>.</p>
<p>The regulation covers services relating to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Preparing the contract.</li>
<li>Formalisation.</li>
<li>Management.</li>
<li>Amendment.</li>
<li>Renewal.</li>
</ul>
<p>It is particularly important to avoid contractual arrangements designed solely to rename the same cost in order to pass it on to the tenant.</p>
<h2>What changes in the annual rent review</h2>
<p>The <strong>Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</strong> also introduces an exceptional restriction relating to certain annual rent reviews.</p>
<p>The measure applies, under the terms established by the regulation, until <strong>31 December 2027</strong>.</p>
<h3>Properties above the maximum reference price</h3>
<p>Where the rent is already above the maximum applicable limit under the reference price system, the regulation provides that no further review should take place during the period affected.</p>
<h3>Other cases</h3>
<p>In other cases, the parties may negotiate the review.</p>
<p>In the absence of an agreement, the legislation establishes a <strong>2%</strong> limit for cases covered by this exceptional measure.</p>
<p>Before automatically applying any percentage, we recommend checking the contract anniversary date, the regime to which it is subject and the applicable reference price.</p>
<h2>Does the Mari Carmen Decree prevent a tenant from being evicted?</h2>
<p><strong>No.</strong></p>
<p>It would be incorrect to say that the Mari Carmen Decree &ldquo;prohibits all evictions until 2030&rdquo;.</p>
<p>RDL 29/2026 introduces specific mechanisms for situations of <strong>vulnerability without alternative housing</strong>, but it distinguishes the treatment depending on who the claimant is and the specific circumstances.</p>
<h3>Certain entities</h3>
<p>The legislation establishes particularly strong protection against certain entities engaged in acquiring properties or mortgage portfolios under the conditions described in the Royal Decree-Law itself.</p>
<p>In certain situations of vulnerability without alternative housing, the suspension may extend until <strong>31 December 2030</strong>.</p>
<h3>Small property owners and other claimants</h3>
<p>This does not mean that the same regime automatically applies to every private owner.</p>
<p>Different rules apply to other claimants, including intervention by the competent authorities, assessment of vulnerability, possible suspensions and, in certain cases, compensation mechanisms.</p>
<p>It is therefore essential to distinguish between a fund, a large-scale landlord and a private owner before determining how the regulation affects proceedings to recover a property.</p>
<h2>What happens if the owner needs to recover their property?</h2>
<p>The answer depends on the contract, its date, its duration and the reason for seeking to terminate it.</p>
<p>There is no general rule under which an owner has automatically lost the right to recover their property.</p>
<p>The LAU maintains certain circumstances and requirements that must be analysed individually.</p>
<p>We must also distinguish between rules currently in force and the amendments envisaged by RDL 28/2026.</p>
<h2>RDL 28/2026 and new rental extensions: what may change on 15 November</h2>
<p>The second block associated with the so-called Mari Carmen Decree regulates the stability of primary residence contracts.</p>
<p>But there is an essential point:</p>
<p><strong>it is due to enter into force on 15 November 2026.</strong></p>
<p>Therefore, as of 18 October, it should not be explained as though it were already the regime in force.</p>
<h3>Successive five- or seven-year extensions</h3>
<p>The published text provides that, once at least five years of the contract have elapsed &mdash;or seven years if the landlord is a legal entity&mdash;, the absence of valid notice of non-renewal would allow new successive extensions of:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Five years</strong> where the landlord is a natural person.</li>
<li><strong>Seven years</strong> where the landlord is a legal entity.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Notice by the owner</h3>
<p>The text provides for <strong>six months&rsquo;</strong> notice for the landlord and retains two months for the tenant in certain cases.</p>
<h3>Compensation for the tenant</h3>
<p>It also introduces a compensation system where the landlord is the party who validly communicates their intention not to continue the contract, with specific exceptions and rules.</p>
<p>It is a highly relevant amendment, but until its entry-into-force date arrives and its final status is confirmed, it should not be confused with the regime currently applicable.</p>

<h2>11-month contracts in Barcelona: are they still valid?</h2>
<p>This is probably one of the searches that will grow most following the entry into force of the new regime.</p>
<p><strong>An 11-month contract is not illegal solely because it lasts 11 months.</strong></p>
<p>The issue arises when this duration is used to give the appearance of a temporary nature that does not really exist.</p>
<p>What matters is determining:</p>
<ul>
<li>Why the tenant needs the property temporarily.</li>
<li>Whether that reason is properly identified.</li>
<li>Whether it can be demonstrated.</li>
<li>Whether the duration is related to the reason.</li>
<li>Whether there are successive contracts that contradict the alleged temporary nature.</li>
</ul>
<p>Therefore, we no longer recommend viewing an 11-month contract as an automatic solution.</p>
<h2>Practical example: a foreign owner with a flat in Eixample</h2>
<p>Imagine an owner living in Germany who owns a flat in Barcelona&rsquo;s Eixample district.</p>
<p>Until now, they had rented the property through successive 11-month seasonal contracts to people who were in fact living permanently in Barcelona.</p>
<p>Under the new framework, they should review that strategy.</p>
<p>If the tenant uses the property to meet a permanent need and there is no genuine, demonstrable temporary reason, simply signing a document entitled &ldquo;temporary contract&rdquo; does not by itself determine its legal nature.</p>
<p>By contrast, if the tenant comes to Barcelona for eight months to carry out a specific professional project and documentation demonstrates that situation, the analysis may be different.</p>
<p>This difference shows why the new regime requires an assessment of <strong>the reality of the tenancy and not just the title of the contract</strong>.</p>
<h2>What happens with tourist accommodation</h2>
<p>The <strong>Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</strong> contains measures that also interact with the short-term accommodation market, but it should not be confused with the specific municipal planning regulations for tourist-use properties.</p>
<p>Barcelona also has its own tourism and planning regulatory framework.</p>
<p>Therefore, anyone who owns a tourist property must jointly analyse:</p>
<ul>
<li>National legislation.</li>
<li>Catalan legislation.</li>
<li>Barcelona municipal regulations.</li>
<li>The property&rsquo;s licence or authorisation.</li>
<li>The applicable tax regime.</li>
<li>Homeowners&rsquo; association rules where applicable.</li>
</ul>
<p>The term Mari Carmen Decree should not be used as though it replaced that entire regulatory framework.</p>
<h2>What does it mean to be a large-scale landlord under the new regulation?</h2>
<p>The legislation retains the general national definition of a <strong>large-scale landlord</strong> as the owner of more than ten urban residential properties or residential built area exceeding 1,500 m&sup2;, excluding garages and storage rooms.</p>
<p>In stressed residential market areas, legislation permits the definition to be adapted to owners of five or more residential properties within the area, where legal requirements are met.</p>
<p>Large-scale landlord status can significantly alter the obligations and procedures that apply.</p>
<p>Therefore, before concluding whether or not a measure affects an owner, we must check their property portfolio and the specific geographical area.</p>

<p>The <strong>Mari Carmen Decree in Barcelona</strong> is not a local rule on coexistence or a minor change in rental regulation.</p>

<p>Its impact is particularly relevant in Barcelona because it is a stressed residential market and has a significant presence of seasonal rentals, international owners and different forms of real estate investment.</p>
